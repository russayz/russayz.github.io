Boot Barn’s final rodeo: why now is the time to go short

The short in brief

Boot Barn is the largest specialty retailer of western and work wear in the US, with 529 stores concentrated in Texas and California. When we pitched it, the stock traded at 22.1 times earnings against a peer average of 16.5 times. That premium assumes flawless store expansion and steady margin gains.

We think the premium is undeserved. New stores increasingly cannibalize existing ones, the core customer is too stretched to absorb price increases, the online business is underbuilt, and maintenance spending has been deferred for years. Together these point to slower same-store sales, rising costs and lower margins. Heavy insider selling and thinning disclosure add to the case.

The trade was not crowded. Short interest was about 10% of the float against a peer average of 18%, with 5.5 days to cover, while 14 of 16 analysts rated the stock a buy.

Where we differ from the Street

ThesisStreet viewOur view

Thesis 1Growth that eats itself

Street view

The runway to 1,200 stores drives sales and economies of scale, and expansion is capital light.

Our view

Our geospatial analysis shows 1 in 4 stores sits within 10 miles of another, 2.5 times peer norms. Acquisitions and new-store ramps flatter same-store sales, and lease liabilities grew 154% from FY21 to FY25.

Thesis 2A customer who cannot absorb price increases

Street view

The core customer is resilient, and exposure to the oil sector is an immaterial legacy risk.

Our view

90% of stores sit in regions with below-median household income, and same-store sales track the US rig count (correlation above 0.8). Tariffs hit Boot Barn’s China-sourced private label harder than peers.

Thesis 3Omnichannel that lags

Street view

Online and stores work together as a digital flywheel that expands margins.

Our view

Online orders run through a single e-commerce center unchanged since 2017 while rivals add distribution centers. A $10 return fee and slow cross-country shipping push digital customers to Ariat and Tecovas.

Thesis 4Hidden costs and thinning disclosure

Street view

Boot Barn is a capital-light retailer, and maintenance capex is immaterial.

Our view

We estimate essential maintenance has been deferred for more than five years, so lease renewals will force remodels. Lease-adjusted free cash flow is already negative, and management stopped reporting its EBITDA reconciliation in FY24.

How we built the view

The work behind the thesis, beyond consensus models and company guidance.

  • Geospatial store analysis

    We geocoded every store and measured overlap: 26.6% sit within 10 miles of another store and 45.6% within 15 miles, with heatmaps for Dallas-Fort Worth and Los Angeles.

  • Customer income mapping

    We matched each store to the median household income of its region, which showed 90% of stores in below-median areas.

  • Oil-cycle sensitivity

    We correlated same-store sales with the Baker Hughes rig count from FY17, a link management stopped discussing (correlation of 0.81).

  • Adjusted same-store sales

    We rebuilt reported comparable sales without e-commerce, acquired stores and new-store ramps to see underlying traffic.

  • Supply chain tracing

    We traced vendor routing to show East Coast, Southeast and Midwest store merchandise ships through the California distribution center.

  • Maintenance capex estimate

    With no disclosed maintenance spend, we estimated the required level from store count and peer disclosures to size the shortfall.

  • Customer and expert input

    We read online reviews of private label against third-party brands and drew on expert interviews, including a former Boot Barn executive.

  • Model build

    A cohort-level revenue model, a lease-adjusted EBITDA build, a five-year DCF, a 10,000-run Monte Carlo simulation and three tiers of comparables.

Catalysts

  • 4Q FY26

    Earnings and price increases

    Management confirmed price increases on exclusive brands began in January. We expect transaction counts to fall and FY27 guidance to land below consensus.

  • 4Q FY26

    Texas drought emergency

    84.5% of Texas is in active drought, including the Permian Basin ranching region where about 20% of stores are located. A 5% same-store sales drag in Texas alone cuts retail same-store sales by about 1 percentage point.

  • 1Q FY27

    Wholesalers dump inventory

    VF Corp sold Dickies and Wolverine is exiting Wolverine Leathers. Liquidated inventory forces more discounting, and we expect 75 to 100 bps of merchandise margin drag.

  • 2Q FY27

    Rivals go public

    A rumored Tecovas IPO would publish faster growth and better returns per store, recasting Boot Barn as a maturing incumbent.

  • 2Q FY27

    New stores disappoint

    As the 2025 and 2026 store vintages reach years two and three, paybacks lengthen and returns fall below the cost of capital, pushing the multiple toward 15 times.

Reshoring lifts workwear demandTariffs are pushing industry back to the US, which supports workwear.Workwear is only about 30% of sales, and rising competition in key markets dilutes Boot Barn’s share of any upturn.
A $200m share buybackBuybacks lift EPS, and executive pay depends heavily on EPS.With lease-adjusted free cash flow negative, buyback capacity is limited, and management has not always followed through.
Private label grows faster than expectedA higher private-label mix would expand margins and blunt the brands’ direct-to-consumer push.Private label is almost entirely sourced overseas, mostly from China, which raises tariff risk, and reviews show weaker quality perception.
