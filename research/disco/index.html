The long in brief

DISCO makes the saws, grinders and polishers that cut and thin wafers into chips, along with the blades, wheels and pads those machines wear through. It holds more than 70% of its global market and earns over 80% of revenue outside Japan, serving TSMC, Samsung, Amkor, Infineon and STMicro.

The Street treats DISCO as a cyclical tool maker whose silicon carbide business depends on electric vehicles. We think three forces arrive sooner than consensus expects: grid investment that needs silicon carbide regardless of EV demand, the shift of 2.5D advanced packaging to outsourced assemblers, and consumables that grow with every wafer processed.

A reverse DCF shows the share price implied free cash flow growth of only 2.75% a year, below Japan’s long-term nominal GDP growth of 2.8%. Even our bear case shows upside.

Where we differ from the Street

ThesisStreet viewOur view

Thesis 1Grid investment drives a silicon carbide supercycle

Street view

Silicon carbide is mainly an EV proxy, so demand is hostage to auto cycles, and the move to 200mm wafers looks slow and uncertain.

Our view

Grid modernization and hyperscaler power upgrades need silicon carbide regardless of EV volatility. Subsidized 200mm lines are tooling now, which front-loads demand for DISCO’s grinders, dicers and polishers.

Thesis 22.5D packaging revives outsourced assemblers

Street view

Analysts focus on hybrid bonding for 3D packaging, which is still early, and assume TSMC keeps 2.5D work in-house.

Our view

Nvidia’s demand forces TSMC to outsource to assemblers, and hyperscalers are working with them on custom chips. 2.5D packaging needs complex wafer preparation and higher-priced equipment.

Thesis 3Consumables compound

Street view

Models emphasize cyclical tool shipments and treat consumables as secondary and tied to capex timing.

Our view

Consumables scale with wafer hours, not capex dates. Silicon carbide’s hardness and thicker wafers raise blade, wheel and tape turnover, and 2.5D adds remount and polish cycles.

How we built the view

The work behind the thesis, beyond consensus models and company guidance.

  • Reverse DCF

    We backed out the growth the share price implied: 2.75% a year in free cash flow, below Japan’s long-term nominal GDP growth.

  • Bottom-up drivers

    We forecast revenue and costs by product line, separating dicers, grinders and recurring consumables.

  • Policy and capex tracking

    We mapped grid capex plans, government aid for 200mm silicon carbide plants and backlogs at grid equipment makers to time demand.

  • Packaging supply chain

    We followed how Nvidia, TSMC and hyperscalers are shifting 2.5D packaging work toward outsourced assemblers, and what that means for tool counts.

  • Comparables

    We built a peer set of back-end semiconductor equipment makers, including Advantest, with explicit selection criteria.

  • Currency and ESG checks

    We tested the valuation across a grid of dollar and euro rates and scored DISCO’s ESG profile against its main competitors.

Valuation

Our five-year base case of ¥54,952 comes from a DCF with a 7.75% WACC and 2.8% terminal growth. DISCO carries no debt, so WACC equals the cost of equity: a 1.58% 10-year JGB yield plus a beta of 1.21 times a 5.10% equity risk premium.

Catalysts

  • Oct 2025

    US grid rules and project awards

    Regulators are finalizing rules for interregional high-voltage DC lines under the $13 billion Transmission Facilitation Program. As projects such as Grain Belt and SunZia begin procurement, demand for silicon carbide inverters inflects.

  • Q1 2026

    Wolfspeed exits bankruptcy

    The largest US silicon carbide producer restructures with long-term supply contracts and government support, restoring confidence in the cycle and in DISCO’s equipment orders.

  • H2 2026

    HBM3E gives way to HBM4

    HBM4 stacks 12 to 16 chips per package against 8 to 12 today, so more wafers pass through dicing and grinding.

  • H1 2027

    Nvidia Rubin launches

    Rubin uses HBM4 and a more complex CoWoS package, straining TSMC’s capacity and sending more work to outsourced assemblers.
